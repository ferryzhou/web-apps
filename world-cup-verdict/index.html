Cross-tournament verdict

The favourite-longshot bias is not a law. It is a weather report.

Across the four settled World Cups, the favourite won 55.1% of the time — 141 of 256 matches. That sounds like a stable fact, but it conceals a fourteen-point swing in accuracy between tournaments: from 45.3% in South Africa 2010 to 59.4% in both Russia 2018 and Qatar 2022. The market is not equally sharp every summer. Some tournaments it reads the form; others it spends a month being surprised.

The deeper finding is that the favourite-longshot bias — the textbook claim that bookmakers under-rate strong favourites and over-rate outsiders — is not a constant. It inverts. In 2010 favourites underperformed their quotes by −3.1%; in 2018 they overperformed by +10.9%. The same market, the same sport, opposite systematic errors four years apart. Anyone betting a single "World Cup edge" across all four tournaments would have found the edge vanish in at least one of them.

What does survive the cross-examination is the confidence filter. In every tournament studied, favourites priced above 60% won markedly more often than those priced barely ahead. The tightest calls — where the favourite sat between 33% and 40% — were a graveyard in three of four tournaments, hitting 0% in Brazil 2014 (zero for twelve). And one strategy, knockout-strong (bet the favourite in knockouts when priced above 50%), was profitable in all four settled tournaments — though on samples so small (two to eight bets each) that the result is a hypothesis, not a licence.

The five studies below each tell the full story for one tournament — accuracy, Brier score, log loss, calibration, and a seven-strategy lab backtested on every match. This page holds them up against each other and asks which lessons travel.

Favourite bias

The signal that inverts.

Favourite bias is observed favourite-win rate minus the rate the odds implied. Positive (green) means favourites won more than priced — the market under-rated them. Negative (red) means they won less — the market over-rated them. The classic favourite-longshot bias says this should be positive. Four tournaments say it can go either way.

The caveat is in the bet counts. Knockout + Strong placed just 3, 7, 2 and 8 bets across the four settled tournaments — a perfect record on a tiny sample. Always Bet Favourite placed 64 each time and finished between −17.1% and +16.6%. The rule that travels most reliably is the one with the least evidence; the rule with the most evidence is the most volatile. That tension is the honest finding.

Calibration

Confidence travels. The dead zone does not.

Grouping matches by the probability the market gave the favourite reveals which bands are stable across tournaments and which swing wildly. Heavy favourites (≥60%) were reliable everywhere; the tightest calls (33–40%) were a coin-flip at best and a wipeout at worst.

The 60–70% band won 64–80% of the time in all four tournaments — the most stable zone in the study. The 33–40% band never exceeded 55% and hit zero in 2014. The lesson that travels: trust heavy favourites, distrust tight calls, and treat everything in between as noise.

Colophon

The verdict is only as good as the evidence behind it.

Method

Decimal odds are converted to implied probabilities, normalised by the overround to remove bookmaker margin, and scored against actual results with accuracy, Brier score, log loss and ranked probability score. Every figure is computed client-side from the embedded dataset in each study.

Data

The four settled studies (2010–2022) use real 90-minute results with representative pre-match odds at a realistic ~5–7% overround. The 2026 study is illustrative — plausible outcomes on plausible odds, ready to be replaced with real data. Knockout ties level at 90 minutes are coded as draws for the 1X2 market.

Scope & limits

256 settled matches is enough to surface calibration patterns but not enough to settle them. Per-bucket rates carry wide intervals at the extremes; strategy ROIs on single-digit bet counts are hypotheses, not certainties. This is an analytical study, not betting advice.
