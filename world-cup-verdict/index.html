Cross-tournament verdict

The favourite-longshot bias is not a law. It is a weather report.

Across the seven settled World Cups, the favourite won 56.7% of the time — 254 of 448 matches. That sounds like a stable fact, but it conceals a twenty-point swing in accuracy between tournaments: from 45.3% in South Africa 2010 to 65.6% in Germany 2006. The market is not equally sharp every summer. Some tournaments it reads the form; others it spends a month being surprised.

The deeper finding is that the favourite-longshot bias — the textbook claim that bookmakers under-rate strong favourites and over-rate outsiders — is not a constant. It inverts. In 2010 favourites underperformed their quotes by −3.1%; in 2006 they overperformed by +15.2%. The same market, the same sport, opposite systematic errors four years apart. Anyone betting a single "World Cup edge" across all seven tournaments would have found the edge vanish in at least two of them.

What does survive the cross-examination is the confidence filter. In every tournament studied, favourites priced above 60% won markedly more often than those priced barely ahead — the 60–70% band converted between 64% and 92% of the time. The tightest calls, where the favourite sat between 33% and 40%, were a graveyard: they never exceeded 55% and hit zero in Brazil 2014. And the sturdiest strategy was not the headline-grabbing one. Strong Favourites (bet the favourite above 50%) was profitable in six of seven tournaments, on 22–33 bets each — more evidence, more reliability than the volatile knockout filters.

The eight studies below each tell the full story for one tournament — accuracy, Brier score, log loss, calibration, and a seven-strategy lab backtested on every match. This page holds them up against each other and asks which lessons travel.

Favourite bias

The signal that inverts.

Favourite bias is observed favourite-win rate minus the rate the odds implied. Positive (green) means favourites won more than priced — the market under-rated them. Negative (red) means they won less — the market over-rated them. The classic favourite-longshot bias says this should be positive. Seven tournaments say it can go either way.

The picture changed when the study reached back to 1998. Knockout + Strong — perfect across the four most recent tournaments — broke even in Korea/Japan 2002 and is no longer flawless. The confidence filter (Strong Favourites ≥50%) is the sturdiest edge: profitable in six of seven, on 22–33 bets each. Always Bet Favourite placed 64 bets every tournament and swung between −17.1% and +17.0%. The lesson: the filter that isolates confidence travels better than the one that chases knockout drama.

Calibration

Confidence travels. The dead zone does not.

Grouping matches by the probability the market gave the favourite reveals which bands are stable across tournaments and which swing wildly. Heavy favourites (≥60%) were reliable everywhere; the tightest calls (33–40%) were a coin-flip at best and a wipeout at worst.

Colophon

The verdict is only as good as the evidence behind it.

Method

Decimal odds are converted to implied probabilities, normalised by the overround to remove bookmaker margin, and scored against actual results with accuracy, Brier score, log loss and ranked probability score. Every figure is computed client-side from the embedded dataset in each study.

Data

The seven settled studies (1998–2022) use real 90-minute results with representative pre-match odds at a realistic ~5–7% overround. The 2026 tournament is complete — Spain beat Argentina in the final — but its study still runs on an illustrative 52-match sample (real bracket outcomes from the semi-finals onward, representative odds throughout), ready to be replaced with the full settled record. Knockout ties level at 90 minutes are coded as draws for the 1X2 market.

Scope & limits

448 settled matches is enough to surface calibration patterns but not enough to settle them. Per-bucket rates carry wide intervals at the extremes; strategy ROIs on single-digit bet counts are hypotheses, not certainties. This is an analytical study, not betting advice.
