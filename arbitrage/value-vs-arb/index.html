The scalable version is value, not arb

Arbitrage and value betting are the same trade — monetizing a wrong price — with one decisive difference: who pays you, and whether they can refuse. This page unpacks why every rich practitioner and every maturing arb product converges on model-vs-market pricing, with the evidence, the math, and a simulator where you can watch the crossover happen. The reframe: same idea, different counterparty

Both strategies start from identical raw material — a price that disagrees with the truth. They differ in which disagreement you trade and who ends up on the other side:

Arbitrage: venue A vs venue B

The gap: two venues disagree with each other. You bet both sides; profit is locked whatever happens.

Your counterparty: the specific venue quoting the worse price. It loses to you identifiably, every time — your account is a list of its mistakes.

Edge source: a fleeting dislocation. It must be raced (minutes pre-match, ~13 seconds in-play) and it dies when either venue updates.

The ceiling: the losing venue can simply stop serving you. Stake limits arrive within weeks–months of consistent winning; capacity per arb is small (1–5% margins); speed competition eats the rest.

Value: your model vs the market

The gap: the market disagrees with reality (as estimated by a better model). You bet one side; profit is statistical, realized over hundreds of bets.

Your counterparty: the aggregate flow of less-informed money, intermediated by an exchange or a sharp book. No single loser can identify you or refuse you — the market as a whole pays.

Edge source: renewable information advantage — better data, better modeling, better discipline. It regenerates every match rather than being consumed.

The ceiling: market liquidity and your model's genuine edge. Both grow with skill and capital instead of shrinking with success.

That's the whole thesis in one sentence: arbitrage requires the loser to keep serving you; value only requires the market to keep existing. One of those is a favor, the other is a fact.

Why arb hits a ceiling — the mechanics

  • Winners get fired. The UK Gambling Commission's 2025 data (first regulator-grade numbers): profitable accounts are ~2× as likely to be restricted; of stake-limited accounts, 58.6% were cut to under 10% of normal max stake — many to under 1%. Books also restrict on arb-shaped behavior before you're even in profit (UKGC).
  • The definitive experiment ended exactly this way. Kaunitz, Zhong & Kreiner (2017) made +8.5% in five months of real money betting mispriced outliers — then their accounts were limited to ~$1 stakes. Their paper's conclusion is the field's epitaph: you can beat the odds, but not the business model (arXiv:1710.02824).
  • Capacity per opportunity is tiny and decaying. Typical margins 1–5%; pre-match arbs last minutes (median ~15 in Marshall's data), in-play ones seconds. Even before limits, edge% × fillable size × frequency is lunch money at retail scale — and the fillable size shrinks as your accounts age.
  • Adverse selection does the rest. As bots race the real gaps, what's left for slower players skews toward traps: stale quotes, palpable errors that will be voided, markets about to be suspended. Winning the race requires infrastructure spend — which is capital converting your "free lunch" into a competitive business with normal returns.

Why value scales — the mechanics

  • The venue wants your action. Exchanges (Betfair, Smarkets, prediction markets like Kalshi/Polymarket) charge commission and profit from volume — they structurally cannot lose to you and never ban winners. Sharp books (the Pinnacle model) run low margins, welcome winners, and use sharp money to correct their own lines — winning customers are their price-discovery mechanism.
  • Edge is renewable, not consumable. An arb consumed is gone. A modeling edge prices next week's matches too. Starlizard reportedly sustains ~3% ROI on billions staked per year — three points that never stop compounding because the edge source is a 160-person research operation, not a dislocation (profile).
  • Size scales with liquidity, not tolerance. A value bettor's stake ceiling is what the market can absorb without moving — which for major football/basketball markets is orders of magnitude above any bookmaker's per-account limit. Walters wagered hundreds of millions per year; his constraint was disguising which bets were his (runners), not being allowed to bet at all (Walters).
  • Compounding favors the statistical edge. Locked profit on a capped stake is linear income that decays; a positive-EV edge sized as a fraction of bankroll is geometric growth. The simulator below makes this concrete.
  • The industry's own behavior is the tell. RebelBetting and OddsJam — companies that sell arb detection — both steer maturing users toward value betting, explicitly because their customers' arb careers end in limits. When the arb vendors' retention strategy is "graduate to value," believe them.

The math of the crossover

Two growth laws. Arbitrage earns a locked margin on a stake that is capped by the venue, and the cap decays as accounts get identified. Value earns an expected margin on a stake that is a fraction of your own bankroll (Kelly sizing), so it compounds:

arb income / week ≈ cycles × min(stake capt, bankroll) × margin,   capt = cap0 × (1 − decay)t
value growth / bet ≈ f × edge,  f = kelly fraction × (p·d − 1)/(d − 1)  → bankroll × (1 + f·edge)bets, with variance

Arb starts faster (a real, locked 1–2% beats a statistical 3% with variance) — that's why everyone starts there. But one curve is linear-then-flat and the other is exponential-with-wobble. Time settles the argument.

The maturation ladder

Almost every documented career and product follows the same rungs — each one trades a lower guaranteed margin for a higher ceiling:

Rungs 1–3 are the same trap with different bait: your counterparty knows your name. The jump from 3 to 4 — proving your edge against the closing line instead of against soft books — is the jump from arb-thinking to value-thinking, and it's where scalability begins.

The self-test: closing line value

How do you know your model edge is real before variance tells you (which takes thousands of bets)? The industry-standard answer: closing line value (CLV). The closing price — the last price before an event starts — is the market's most information-complete estimate, and sharp books' own test of a customer. If the prices you bet consistently beat the close (you bet 2.10 and it closes 1.95), you are ahead of the market's information flow and profit follows; if not, your "edge" is noise. Kaunitz et al.'s strategy worked precisely because deviations from consensus predicted the close. CLV is also the honest bridge back to arbitrage thinking: an arb is just a bet guaranteed to beat somebody's close — value betting generalizes the same comparison to the whole market.

Caveats before you fall in love. (1) Real model edges are rare and small — Winkelmann et al. 2024 show most published betting-market "inefficiencies" are small-sample artifacts; assume your backtest is fooling you until CLV says otherwise. (2) Variance is brutal: a genuine 3% edge at even odds still produces losing months regularly; under-sized Kelly (¼ or less) exists because full Kelly with an overestimated edge goes broke. (3) The scalable venues charge for scale — exchange commission (2–5%) and prediction-market fees are the new "cost of the counterparty," and thin edges die there just like arbs died at Kalshi's fee in our case study. (4) This page is analysis, not encouragement: the honest base rate is that most people attempting rung 4 don't have an edge.

Beyond betting: the same law everywhere

  • Crypto: cross-exchange arb (rung 2) was raced to sub-fee levels years ago; the survivors run market-making and basis/funding strategies (rungs 4–5) — statistical edges on flow, not locked gaps.
  • Equities: classic pairs/ADR arbitrage compressed decades ago; stat-arb and market-making firms (the Citadels and Janes) are rung-5 institutions: they quote, others cross the spread.
  • Retail flips: clearance arbitrage (rung 2) caps at your labor; brands and private-label sellers (rung 5) own the price-setting side of the same marketplace.
  • The general law: a locked profit requires an identifiable loser who must knowingly keep paying you — so it gets refused, raced, or repriced. A statistical edge is paid by aggregate flow that never meets you — so it scales until liquidity or skill runs out. Unequal prices fund both; only one survives success.
