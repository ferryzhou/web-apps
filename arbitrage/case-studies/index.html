Betting Market Arbitrage — what actually happens when people try

Two first-hand case studies built from real, timestamped odds data in this repo, plus the published record: academic papers, the famous experiments, the commercial arb industry, and the syndicates who got rich doing something subtly different. Every number here traces to a linked source. Siblings: strategy lab · spread monitor.

Part 1 · Our own case studies (primary data)

Case study A — Argentina vs Cape Verde, WC 2026 R32: the fee-sized mirage

We compiled timestamped 3-way odds from six source types (books, media-quoted lines, a prediction market) for one match. Combining the best price per outcome across venues produced an implied total of 99.62% on match day — a genuine 0.38% cross-venue arbitrage on paper, driven by Kalshi pricing the draw and the underdog cheaper than sportsbooks. Applying Kalshi's ≈7%·P·(1−P) taker fee moved the total to 100.57%: the entire edge was smaller than one venue's fee. Meanwhile the "wrong" outcome hit — a 90-minute draw paying +650 to +750 — a reminder that the payoff distribution, not the realized result, is what a strategy should be judged on.

Case study B — Round of 16 live scan, Jul 6 2026: near-arbs cluster at the venue boundary

Scanning four upcoming knockout games across books + prediction markets found no fat riskless arb, but three combinations within ~0.3% of break-even, all sportsbook-vs-prediction-market: Kalshi Spain-to-advance 64¢ + FanDuel Portugal +180 → 99.7% (pre-fee); FanDuel USA −106 + DraftKings Belgium +105 → 100.24% (a stale-line window after the Balogun reinstatement news moved lines at different speeds); Switzerland priced 25–26% by both prediction markets vs 28.6% implied at books. Lesson: gaps live at structural boundaries (different customer bases, fee models, regulation) and around news, and most disappear once you demand contemporaneous timestamps — three of our scraped quotes failed basic sanity checks.

Case study C — in-play volatility (the phantom-arb trap)

In-play is where phantom arbs cluster. Right after a goal, a book that reprices late shows up as a huge phantom "arb": in synthetic 5-minute multi-book snapshots, an apparent 28% edge appeared purely from a 150-second timestamp mismatch. A staleness filter kills the phantoms; whatever survives it with tight timestamps is the real in-play opportunity set — which the Princeton study below measured at ~4.5% of game time, ~13 seconds per window.

Part 2 · The academic record

Peer-reviewed (or noted otherwise), oldest to newest. The pattern: arbs exist, they're small, they close fast, and the best-documented constraint is not price discovery but bookmaker behavior.

StudyMarket / sampleKey finding
Hausch & Ziemba 1990, J. Business US horse racing, cross-track parimutuel pools The founding example: the same race priced differently by isolated pools at different tracks allowed risk-free cross-track arbitrage.
Vlastakis, Dotsis & Markellos 2009, J. Forecasting European football, six online bookmakers Cross-bookmaker arbitrage: "limited but highly profitable" — rare in frequency, high per-arb return; simple trading rules also beat the market, contradicting weak-form efficiency.
Marshall 2009, QREF Sports betting arbitrage dataset How fast do arbs close? Median duration ≈ 15 minutes, longest just under a day (pre-match, pre-automation era).
Franck, Verbeek & Nüesch 2013, Economica Top-5 European soccer leagues, bookmakers vs Betfair exchange Back-at-bookmaker + lay-at-exchange locked a profit in 19.2% of matches. Books often posted arbable prices at negative margin — interpreted as deliberate loss-leader pricing, not error.
Gómez-González & del Corral 2018, Econ. & Business Letters 5 major books, 7 leagues, 2000–2017 Overrounds fell continuously for 17 years and surebet frequency rose — competition mechanically creates more cross-book arbs even among majors.
Winkelmann et al. 2024, J. Sports Economics Simulations + 14 football seasons The skeptic's paper: small samples routinely "find" inefficiency in efficient markets; real inefficiencies appear in single seasons but are not persistent. Treat every backtest headline with suspicion.
Princeton thesis ~2025 not peer-reviewed Live NBA moneylines, FanDuel vs BetMGM In-play arbs existed in 4.52% of scraped game time with typical windows of ~13 seconds — confirming that live opportunities are real but demand automation.
arXiv 2605.00864 (2026) pointer only — unverified Polymarket NBA markets Recent preprint analyzing arbitrage within prediction-market sports contracts; listed here as a starting point for the newest venue class.

Part 3 · The famous experiment: “Beating the bookies with their own numbers” (2017)

Kaunitz, Zhong & Kreiner (arXiv:1710.02824) used no forecasting model at all — they treated the consensus of many bookmakers' odds as the true probability and bet single legs wherever one book's price deviated enough. It is the cleanest public test of "profit from unequal prices" in betting, and its ending is the industry's defining fact. Code and data are public on GitHub.

Their conclusion was not "markets are efficient" — it was that you can beat the odds but not the business model: bookmakers correct discrimination against winners faster than they correct prices. (Caveats: never peer-reviewed; the 5-month live sample is statistically thin.)

Additional practitioner-documented failure modes: palpable-error voids (a book cancels its "obvious mistake" leg, leaving you naked on the other — any arb over ~10–15% is presumptively an error), line moves between legs, and KYC/withdrawal friction spreading bankroll across many books.

The tell: every commercial arb product eventually pushes maturing users away from pure arbitrage — toward value betting or promo extraction — because their customers hit the account-limit ceiling. The vendors monetize the search; the ceiling is yours.

Part 6 · The people who actually got rich did something different

The best-documented winners are value bettors, not arbitrageurs: they built models that out-predict the market, took directional risk, and engineered execution around the winner-limiting problem.

  • Tony Bloom / Starlizard — ~160-staff London analytics firm; reportedly ~3% ROI on billions staked in Asian handicap markets, including automated in-play betting. Owns Brighton & Hove Albion.
  • Matthew Benham / Smartodds — ex-City trader applying derivatives discipline to football probabilities; bought Brentford FC with the proceeds.
  • Billy Walters / The Computer Group — reportedly ~57% against the spread over decades, hundreds of millions wagered per year through networks of "runners" — needed precisely because books refuse a known winner's action.

The through-line with pure arb: both monetize unequal prices (model price vs market price, instead of venue A vs venue B), but value betting scales because it doesn't require being paid by the venue that's wrong about you — only by the market. That's why the syndicates own the endgame.

Part 7 · The newest boundary: prediction markets vs sportsbooks (2024–2026)

Kalshi and Polymarket adding sports contracts created a fresh venue boundary with segmented customer bases (US retail vs global crypto) — practitioner sources report persistent 1–5% gross gaps, bot-executed in milliseconds (overview, cross-platform tools, open-source bots). Two structural differences from bookmaker arb: exchanges don't ban winners, but resolution-criteria mismatch replaces palpable-error risk — the "same" event worded differently can settle differently (our strategy lab calls this the same-claim test). This matches our own R16 scan, where every near-arb sat exactly on this boundary. Peer-reviewed literature hasn't caught up yet; treat the numbers as practitioner-reported.

Synthesis — seven lessons

  1. Paper arbs are common; money arbs are rare. 19.2% of matches had a bookmaker-exchange arb (Franck 2013); typical margins are 1–5%; anything fatter is probably a pricing error that will be voided.
  2. Speed is the price of admission. Pre-match arbs last minutes (median ~15, Marshall 2009); in-play arbs last ~13 seconds (Princeton ~2025). Our in-play tooling shows most apparent live arbs are one stale book.
  3. Fees are first-order, not rounding. Our Case A edge (0.38%) was smaller than one venue's fee; exchange commission (2–5%) decides whether the Franck-style arb exists at all.
  4. The real ceiling is being allowed to play. Winners are ~2× as likely to be restricted (UKGC 2025); Kaunitz et al. were limited to $1 stakes within months. Plan for the ceiling before the first bet.
  5. Gaps live at structural boundaries and around news. Different regulation, fees, and customer bases (books vs exchanges vs prediction markets) sustain gaps; news (a player reinstated) reprices venues at different speeds.
  6. Distrust your own backtest. Winkelmann 2024: single-season "inefficiencies" are often sampling noise. Demand persistence across seasons and contemporaneous timestamps.
  7. The scalable version is value, not arb. Every rich practitioner and every maturing arb product converges on model-vs-market pricing — same idea (unequal prices), different counterparty. Full deep dive on this point →
