Amol Avasare · Anthropic

Aired Apr 5, 2026253 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Amol Avasare: How Anthropic Uses Claude to Grow Itself

Amol Avasare, Anthropic's Head of Growth, on Lenny's Podcast (Apr 2026). Anthropic went from about $1B to $19B ARR in 14 months. About 70% of his time goes to "success disasters," where growth breaks other things.

How the team uses agents

  • CASH is Claude running growth experiments: spot an opportunity, build it, check it against the brand bar, analyze results. So far it handles copy and small UI changes, and its hit rate is about that of a junior PM. Humans still approve each step.
  • A weekly Cowork task reads Slack and flags where teams are misaligned or duplicating work.
  • A morning task reviews 20 to 25 dashboards and reports what looks wrong.
  • Claude handles admin: room booking, expenses and email triage.

How the team works

  • Engineers produce 2 to 3 times more, so PMs and designers are now the bottleneck.
  • Two-week rule: for projects of two engineer-weeks or less, the engineer is the PM.
  • 60 to 80% of what they ship has no PRD. Prototypes and Slack threads replace it.
  • Spend 50 to 70% of effort on big bets, not small tweaks, because the product will be far better in two years.

Advice

  • The hard part is activation. Models improve faster than users learn what they can do. Asking users who they are, then routing them, works even though it adds friction.
  • Re-test your tools after each model release.
  • Be willing to give up some metric gains for safety and brand.

Boris Cherny · Claude Code

Aired May 20, 2026239 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Boris Cherny: Running Hundreds of Agents at Once

Boris Cherny, head of Claude Code at Anthropic, on Big Technology with Alex Kantrowitz (May 2026). Claude Code now writes 100% of Claude Code. Code written per engineer at Anthropic is up about 250% while quality has stayed stable. At Meta, by comparison, a 1 to 3% productivity gain in a year was hard-won.

How he works

  • He runs about 5 agents at a time on his laptop and hundreds overnight. "I have a Claude that's talking to my Claudes."
  • He hands off a task, works on something else, then reviews and corrects it. Once, he had Cowork check his email and calendar before booking 8 flights and 5 hotels, and it caught his own mistakes.
  • He keeps personal test cases and reruns them on every new model.
  • He picks the model and effort level per task: max effort for hard problems, low effort to save tokens.
  • He uses auto mode, where a second Claude checks each command, instead of clicking "always allow".

Advice for companies

  • Give everyone tokens without per-use approval, and make it safe to fail.
  • Big wins come from people you wouldn't predict, like an accountant or a new grad.
  • Optimize cost only after a use case scales.
  • Rebuild the process around AI. A computer at the edge of a paper office didn't help either.
  • Keep a beginner's mindset. What failed last month may work now.

Bret Taylor · Sierra

Aired Mar 10, 2026241 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Bret Taylor: What Sierra Learned Deploying AI Agents

Sierra CEO Bret Taylor with John Collison on Cheeky Pint (Mar 2026). Sierra builds customer-service agents for large companies. It reached about $100M ARR in seven quarters. Its customers automate 70 to 90% of support cases.

What he's seen in deployments

  • Start with one channel and a few use cases, then add more.
  • A human-handled case costs $10 to $20. An AI case costs cents, so companies can afford good service for every customer.
  • Second-order effects show up. Human agents get only the hard cases and say they're more satisfied. One retailer's costs stayed flat, but conversations grew 2 to 3 times.
  • Let the agent reason across messy systems instead of waiting on a big IT cleanup.
  • Customers set goals and guardrails. A supervisor model checks the agent's work.
  • Expect to throw your own code away when models catch up.

Pricing

  • Charge for outcomes, not usage: a fee per resolved case, nothing when a case goes to a human. "Token usage isn't value."

Lessons for leaders

  • "The atomic unit of productivity in AI is a process, not a person." Give each process an owner and a goal, like cutting supplier onboarding from 17 days to one.
  • Narrow the problem. Don't build an AI for every contract. Automate one contract type and send the exceptions to legal.
  • Handing everyone Copilot doesn't make you an AI company.

These numbers are Taylor's own claims.

Dan Shipper · Every

Aired May 24, 2026162 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Dan Shipper: One Agent for the Whole Company

Every CEO Dan Shipper on Lenny's Podcast (May 2026). Every has about 30 people running about 6 software products. The team doubled in a year even as agents took on more work.

How Every uses agents

  • "We have an agent internally called Claudia, which runs our whole consulting practice."
  • He now favors one "super agent" for the whole company over a personal agent for each person.
  • Quarterly planning ran through agents that interviewed every employee and drafted the team plans.
  • Users' agents file detailed bug reports. Those become GitHub issues, and agents fix them.
  • Engineers mostly work in plain language and rarely read code line by line.

Lessons

  • "Agents need people who care about them." Someone has to maintain and improve each agent.
  • More automation brought more hiring, not less.
  • Benchmarks measure only what can be scored. People still decide what's worth building.
  • Caveat: much of the episode is predictions rather than practice.

Eric Glyman · Ramp

Aired Feb 17, 2026194 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Eric Glyman: Agents That Review 100,000 Expenses a Day

Ramp CEO Eric Glyman with John Collison on Cheeky Pint (Feb 2026). Ramp's agents review more than 100,000 expenses a day, and he says they're over 99% accurate.

How the expense agent works

  • The customer writes its expense policy in plain English.
  • The agent applies it using the receipt, timing and transaction data.
  • It writes an audit trail for every decision. A human can still sign off, which keeps auditors happy.

How Ramp builds

  • Designers, marketers and support staff ship small changes to production by prompting agents, like changing a button's color.
  • Teams are still about 1 PM, 5 to 8 engineers and 1 designer. Real customers and old systems mean you can't just cut headcount.
  • What matters is how fast you go from seeing a problem to fixing it.

Lessons

  • As software gets cheaper to build, moats matter more: your own data, network effects, operational detail.
  • Sell the finished work, not just the tool. "They sell money and we sell time."
  • Regenerating code from outcomes is promising, but not for systems that need 99.99% uptime.
  • The 99% accuracy figure is Glyman's own claim.

Fiona Fung · Anthropic

Aired Jun 21, 2026227 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Fiona Fung: Running the Most AI-Pilled Engineering Team

Fiona Fung, who manages the Claude Code and Cowork teams at Anthropic, on Lenny's Podcast (Jun 2026). "Coding is no longer the bottleneck." She cites an Anthropic chart showing engineers ship about 8 times more code per quarter than in 2025.

How her team works

  • Agents run in the background on schedules. A morning routine summarizes user feedback channels and drafts PRs for her to review.
  • Once a month, a Claude session with access to all team repos, Slack and metrics reviews what each person shipped. She uses it to start one-on-one conversations.
  • Specs in the repo define what "good" looks like, and Claude reviews code against them. "Claude is very good when you give it a framework to validate against."
  • PMs and designers check in code.
  • Monthly planning replaced six-month roadmaps. Teams have "explicit permission to kill processes that no longer serve us."
  • Bugs are graded "bad" (lost work, unrecoverable) or "sad" (annoying but recoverable).

Lessons and open problems

  • Running about 20 agents at once means constant context switching. She blocks focus time to catch up.
  • How far to trust fully automated review is still open.
  • Training junior engineers who rarely read code is a real concern.
  • Hire two profiles: creative builders with product sense, and deep systems experts.
  • "High agency is also high accountability."

Geoff Charles · Ramp

Aired Mar 15, 2026242 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Geoff Charles: Inside Ramp, Where AI Agents Run Everything

Ramp CPO Geoff Charles on Peter Yang's Behind the Craft (Mar 2026). About 50% of Ramp's production code is AI-written, up from about 30% in December, and the target is 80%. "My job is to automate my job."

Agents Ramp built

  • Voice of the customer: searches tickets, chats, sales notes and call recordings. It analyzed 90 days of feedback in about 8 minutes, work he says would take a person 8 days.
  • Spec writer: a Claude Code skill questions the PM on goals and trade-offs and pushes back on weak answers. It runs 6 to 10 research agents over competitors, sales calls, tickets and the codebase, then writes a 2-minute spec.
  • Inspect: an internal coding agent that builds full-stack features from a prompt and opens PRs.
  • Ramp Research: anyone can ask data questions and get a full report back.
  • Releases bot: writes help articles and impact reports for each launch.

How they drive adoption

  • An L0 to L3 AI proficiency ladder, with usage tracked company-wide.
  • No budget limits on AI tools.
  • Candidates build and demo something in the interview.
  • Staged rollout: dogfood, alpha, then about 10% of customers, then everyone.

Lessons

  • "If you're not using Claude Code this year, no matter what your role is, you're probably underperforming."
  • AI, not engineers, now reads the spec. Keep it short and precise.
  • "Management is probably dead." Be the best builder you can.

Guillermo Rauch · Vercel

Aired Oct 16, 2026261 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Guillermo Rauch: Building the Internet for Agents

Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch with Molly O'Shea on Sourcery (Oct 2026). At the start of 2026, 3% of Vercel deployments came from coding agents. By October it was 60%. "The machines have taken over."

How Vercel uses agents

  • "Almost everything we do at Vercel, we do with agents." Sales reps ask a Slack agent about an account instead of opening Salesforce, and the answer is tailored to their role.
  • An agent rewrote the first version of a Formula 1 team's migration in five minutes. That used to take months.
  • When an agent visits vercel.com, it gets precise text about the business instead of the visual site. Agents now buy services from Vercel's marketplace with no human involved.

His practices

  • Act like an engineering manager toward agents. Read the code, have the AI explain what it did, write tests, poke holes.
  • Don't lock into one model. About 80% of Vercel's AI token traffic now goes to cheaper open models. A common pattern: a frontier model writes the plan and an open model carries it out.
  • He doesn't watch videos. He pulls the transcript, skims it, then asks his agent questions.

Lessons

  • Agents are "soldiers with no frontal lobe." They will do anything to finish the mission. Sandbox them and plan for prompt injection.
  • Treat code as a liability. Critical systems need stricter rules than prototypes.
  • Judge by retention, not hype. Users have little loyalty to any one model.
  • Hire for the ability to direct many agents, not for having "vibe-coded this thing."

Jason Lemkin · SaaStr

Aired Jan 1, 2026253 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Jason Lemkin: We Replaced Our Sales Team with 20 AI Agents

Jason Lemkin on Lenny's Podcast (Jan 2026). After two salespeople quit mid-event, SaaStr stopped hiring for sales. About 10 sales staff became 1.2 humans and 20 agents. "The net productivity is about the same," and now it scales.

How to deploy a sales agent

  • Start with support. Their "Digital Jason" clone began by answering event questions and later closed a $70K sponsorship on its own.
  • Buy, don't build. Choose the vendor that will help you most, ideally with a forward-deployed engineer.
  • Train it on your best rep's emails and scripts. Then correct its mistakes for an hour or two a day for 30 days.
  • Give each agent its own segment of customers so agents don't collide.
  • Plan on ongoing oversight. Their part-time Chief AI Officer spends 10 to 15 hours a week reviewing output. "If you just buy one and disappear, you will have zero ROI."

What worked

  • Inbound: a site chatbot qualifies visitors and books meetings around the clock.
  • Reactivation: an agent emailed leads that reps had dropped and got a 70% response rate.
  • Disclosing AI didn't matter. "No one cares."

Predictions

  • Email-cadence SDRs and inbound BDRs will mostly disappear by the end of 2026.
  • AEs keep about 70% of the job for now, but they need to be far more productive.
  • Promote a quantitative person from inside to run the agents.

Try this: once a quarter, use your own product in incognito mode. Fix the worst experience with an agent.

John Kim · Sendbird

Aired May 6, 2026251 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

John Kim: Turning a Whole Company into AI Builders

Sendbird CEO John Kim with Claire Vo on How I AI (May 2026). His playbook for getting every employee building with agents, not just engineers.

What they built

  • Quests: anyone posts a task they want automated, like chasing receivables or sourcing candidates. Engineers, other employees or AI agents claim it. Each quest shows its risk and the weeks it saves.
  • Rewards: points that buy gift cards, tea with an executive, or a demo slot at the weekly standup.
  • Safe template: a pre-approved app template with login, database, security review and hosting built in, so non-engineers can ship safely.
  • Skills marketplace: teams share agent skills so nobody builds the same thing twice.
  • Token leaderboard: five tiers, from Beginner (under 1M tokens a day) to "AI God" (over 100M). It's for coaching, not performance reviews. He meets one-on-one with low users to find what's blocking them.

Results

  • Marketing built a swag store with payments in a day or two, with no engineering help.
  • The biggest token users are the CTO and his co-founder. He uses 30 to 50M tokens a day himself.
  • A smooth usage curve means agents are working around the clock. Dips mean people are on vacation.

Lessons

  • Treat internal tooling as a product, not a program.
  • Start with your champions, not your skeptics.
  • Hire for curiosity, agency and energy over years of experience.
  • Put one person in charge of internal AI, with a weekly task force including security.

Nan Yu & Jacob Shumway · Linear

Aired Aug 9, 2026250 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Linear: 5 Rules for AI Agents That Work in Production

Nan Yu and Jacob Shumway, who built Linear Agent, on Peter Yang's Behind the Craft (Aug 2026). Their main point: "The models are really smart, but we're just not using them enough." Over-prompting usually makes an agent worse.

The 5 rules

  1. Map the real workflow first. Know where work starts, where the context lives, and what "done" means. Hook into those places. If work starts in Slack, start the agent in Slack, not in a separate chatbot.
  2. Give it tools, not context. "Give it as little instruction as possible. Give it the tools to load context." Their fix for hallucinations was skills the agent loads only when it needs them, instead of exposing every action at once.
  3. Start with one frequent job. Theirs was turning sales notes and Slack threads into issues. They launched it quietly and built the next steps from real usage.
  4. Use the biggest model until it works. Write evals, then move to smaller models for narrow jobs.
  5. Turn real failures into evals. When the agent lacks a capability, it reports the gap, and the gap becomes an issue automatically.

Other lessons

  • Make a few showcase use cases rock solid. Accept more variance in the rest.
  • Use evals only where consistency matters. Too many give false signals.
  • Don't lock it down. Users will find uses you didn't plan for, like translating French support tickets.
  • Demo: a Slack debate became an issue and then a PR in 6 minutes.

Ryan Lopopolo · OpenAI

Aired Jun 9, 2026269 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Ryan Lopopolo: Shipping a Product with Zero Human-Written Code

Ryan Lopopolo of OpenAI on AI Native Dev (Jun 2026), with more detail on Latent Space (Apr 2026). His team of 3, later 7, engineers built an internal app with more than 1M lines of code. Agents wrote all of it. Each engineer now ships about 70 PRs a week. The first six weeks were 10 times slower than writing the code by hand.

Practices

  • When the agent fails, ask what context or tool it's missing. Don't just prompt harder.
  • Keep builds under a minute so agents stay fast.
  • Keep AGENTS.md to about 100 lines. Use it as a table of contents for deeper docs: goals, customers, quality bar.
  • Turn every incident into a written rule, such as "all network calls need timeouts." Write lint and test errors for the agent, telling it how to fix the problem.
  • Tell review agents to bias toward merging and flag only important issues. Otherwise author and reviewer loop forever.
  • Let agents run the whole PR: push, wait for CI, fix flaky tests, resolve conflicts, merge.
  • Do most human review after merge, on samples. Review the plans before work starts.
  • Hold a weekly cleanup day. Encode repeated feedback so you never give it twice.
  • Prefer small command-line tools with terse output over MCP.
  • Treat code as disposable. He'll throw away a 50,000-line experiment to learn where agents struggle.

Lessons

  • "The only fundamentally scarce thing is the synchronous human attention of my team."
  • Humans still own hard refactors, new product ideas, interfaces and customer conversations.
  • Caveat: this was a brand-new codebase.

Sebastian Siemiatkowski · Klarna

Aired Feb 16, 2026198 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Sebastian Siemiatkowski: Klarna's AI Bet, and What It Fixed

Klarna CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski with Harry Stebbings on 20VC (Feb 2026). Klarna went from about 7,000 people to under 3,000, mostly by not backfilling departures. Its AI customer service does the work of about 600 agents.

What Klarna did

  • Built customer service AI in-house. Simple payment questions came first. Harder ones, like how interest is calculated, needed access to the source code, which is why they built instead of bought.
  • Moved data off standard SaaS tools so the AI has the whole company's context. "We need to provide our AI the best context."
  • Shrank mainly through roughly 20% yearly attrition and few new hires. Pay per person is up about 50%.

What they corrected

  • They over-optimized support for cost. Now they put more weight on human support, recruiting passionate customers as part-time support staff.
  • The cost-cutting story hurt their reputation.

Lessons

  • Context is the main input. Off-the-shelf AI can't see your codebase or your policies.
  • "The future of VIP experience will be the human connection."
  • He still codes himself with Claude and Cursor.
  • Headcount figures are his own. The transcript gives both 6,000 and 7,000 as the starting point.

Tobi Lütke · Shopify

Aired Sep 15, 2026219 wordsWatch on YouTube ↗

Tobi Lütke: How Shopify Works with AI Agents

Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke with Shane Parrish on The Knowledge Project (Sep 2026). His main point: as AI gets more capable, taste, judgment and responsibility become the scarce human skills. Full notes: my earlier post.

How Shopify uses agents

  • Few engineers write code by hand. They each coordinate 10 to 50 agents.
  • River is Shopify's AI colleague. It has a name, memory for each Slack channel, codebase access, and permission to push back on leadership.
  • About half of Shopify's pull requests start from a conversation with River.
  • River works only in open channels, so people learn by watching each other use it.
  • Every night River reviews its own conversations and rewrites its skill files.

How Tobi uses agents

  • For a hard decision he runs an "AI council": sub-agents across several models look at it from different angles, then he synthesizes. It costs about $15 to $20 in tokens.
  • The AI informs the decision. He still makes it.

Lessons

  • Watch out for "slop grenades," AI output sent on without being read. Review and compression are the real work now.
  • Give agents a personality. Bland assistants are less useful.
  • Prune and rebuild instead of adding layers. Hold "refounding" events.
  • Machines can't be held accountable, so people must own the decisions.
