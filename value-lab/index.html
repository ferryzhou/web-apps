Value lab · model-vs-market pricing, beyond betting

Can prediction beat the market?

The value approach — bet/trade when your predicted probability disagrees with the market's price — presumes the answer is sometimes yes. This lab collects the actual evidence across domains (betting, prediction markets, equities, commodities, macro), the practitioner playbook, and two tools that keep you honest: how much data it takes to prove an edge, and what your model signal is worth once you respect the market as a prior. Related: value vs arbitrage · arbitrage suite.

First, respect the opponent: a price is a prediction

A market price isn't a rival forecaster — it's the aggregate of every forecaster who was willing to stake money, weighted by conviction and bankroll, error-corrected in real time. Beating it means knowing something the marginal dollar doesn't. Theory says this is possible but self-limiting (Grossman & Stiglitz 1980: prices can't be perfectly efficient, or nobody would be paid to make them efficient — the profit of the informed is the wage of price discovery). The empirical question is where the wage is still being paid, and to whom.

Why the market usually wins

· It already contains the models — every public signal is someone's input.

· Aggregation beats individuals: crowd averages cancel idiosyncratic error (wisdom-of-crowds).

· Skin in the game filters conviction; talk is free, prices aren't.

· Late prices absorb early information: the closing line has seen every bet before it.

Where models can win

· Private or faster data the marginal dollar doesn't have (satellite counts, sensor data, house polls).

· Biased flow to lean against: favorite-longshot bias, home-team money, partisan election money.

· Thin/young markets where the aggregation hasn't happened yet — few pros, low liquidity, new contract types.

· Structural constraints on other participants: fees, position limits, mandates, geography.

The pattern: the deeper and older the market, the more it wins. Models beat markets at the edges — young venues, thin liquidity, biased flow, private data — and those wins are real but local and usually temporary.

The evidence, in detail

Sports: the closing line is the wall

Kovalchik's comparison of eleven published tennis models found none beat bookmaker-implied probabilities (JQAS 2016); the bookmaker-consensus model hit ~72% accuracy vs ~70% for the best Elo-style model. In football, models can match odds-implied accuracy but bookmaker odds are better calibrated, and paper profits appear only at best-of-market historical prices (Wilkens 2026). The one well-documented breach — Kaunitz et al. betting mispriced outliers against the consensus for +8.5% in real money exact ROI paper-internal — was closed by account limiting, not by prices (arXiv:1710.02824). Hence the practitioner benchmark: skill = consistently beating the closing line, nothing less.

Elections: nearly a tie, and instructive in both directions

The Iowa Electronic Markets were closer to the outcome than contemporaneous polls 74% of the time across five presidential cycles (Berg et al. 2008) — but Erikson & Wlezien showed bias-adjusted polls beat the markets, i.e. markets ≈ polls plus adjustment, not magic (POQ/Electoral Studies). Multi-cycle comparisons of FiveThirtyEight vs betting markets (2008–2024) find them "strikingly similarly accurate": markets clearly won 2020 and 2024, the model won 2022 (Lott's deep dive, NBER WP 33339). 2024's market "win" carries an asterisk: a meaningful part of the price was one trader's private neighbor-polling data — a model beating the market through the market.

Superforecasters: the cleanest head-to-head we have

Tetlock's Good Judgment Project won IARPA's 4-year forecasting tournament outright (track record). The decisive experiment: over two seasons and 261 geopolitical events, raw forecast-poll averages lost to a real prediction market — but statistically aggregated team forecasts (performance-weighted, time-decayed, extremized) beat the market (Atanasov et al., Management Science 2017). So prediction can beat the market — when it's better aggregation, not a lone genius. The famous "~30% better than intelligence analysts with classified data" figure traces to a single 2013 journalistic account of unpublished results unverified at the number level. Community trackers currently score Metaculus's aggregate among the best-calibrated public forecasts (Brier ≈ 0.11 on resolved questions) alongside real-money markets community analyses, not peer-reviewed.

Equities: the brutal base rate, and the exceptions that price it

SPIVA year-end 2024: 65% of active US large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500 that year, ~64% is the 24-year annual average, and 89.5% underperformed over 15 years; persistence is below chance — under 1% of top-quartile funds stayed top-quartile five years running (SPIVA, Persistence Scorecard). The exceptions define the rule's price: Renaissance's Medallion made ~66% gross / ~39% net annually for three decades with no losing year — and stayed capped near $10B, closed to outside money (Zuckerman, summary); and "Buffett's Alpha" is largely explained by early factor identification plus ~1.7:1 cheap insurance-float leverage — systematic insight, not clairvoyant stock-picking (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen).

Commodities, rates, weather: markets win on price, models win on private inputs

Fed funds futures out-forecast economist surveys at short horizons (Gürkaynak, Sack & Swanson); livestock futures beat USDA price forecasts, though USDA keeps its edge on quantity forecasts where it owns the data (Sanders & Manfredo 2003). Weather is the live frontier: better forecasts demonstrably convert to trading gains in power markets (Kath & Ziel), and funds run in-house weather desks — a model edge that exists precisely because it's private, and that becomes the price as it trades realized fund P&L not publicly documented.

Known market biases: real, persistent, and mostly unprofitable

Markets misprice in documented directions: across 5.6M US horse-race starts, expected return fell from ~85¢/$ on even-money favorites to ~55¢/$ on 50-to-1 longshots — the favorite-longshot bias, persistent for decades (Snowberg & Wolfers, JPE 2010). Bookmakers know and deliberately shade lines into bettor biases, earning more than the vig (Levitt, EJ 2004). The catch: most biases are smaller than the take — they tell you what not to bet more than what to bet. And prediction markets have their own failure modes worth discounting: thin liquidity, whales moving prices (Théo's positions were a large share of 2024 depth), reported wash trading fraction unverified, and fee/cap-induced tail distortions (Fortune).

Tool 1 · The edge-proof calculator — skill or luck?

Before believing any edge (yours or a tipster's): how many bets does it take for a true edge to be statistically distinguishable from luck? Frequentist answer for flat stakes:

n ≈ ((zα + zβ) · σ / e)²   σ² = p·(d−1)² + (1−p) − e²  (per-unit-stake return variance)

This is why closing line value exists as a shortcut (see playbook below): CLV correlates with true edge and shows up in dozens of bets instead of thousands. It's also why single-season backtests “finding” edges should be assumed noise (Winkelmann et al. 2024).

Tool 2 · The humility blender — market as prior, model as signal

The professional default is not "trust my model" but "start from the market and tilt." Blend in log-odds space with a weight that reflects your proven track record, then see whether any bet survives the venue's actual payout:

logit(blend) = (1 − w) · logit(market fair prob) + w · logit(model prob)  →  EV = blend × offered odds − 1

Try it: a model that says 55% when the market says 50% — a big disagreement — is worth almost nothing after a modest weight and a 2.5% margin. This one interaction explains most value-betting failure: the signal was real but smaller than the costs. It also shows what does work: either a much better model (w → 1 with proof) or a venue with less margin (exchanges, sharp books).

The practitioner playbook

How people who do this for real actually operate — the consensus workflow, with the numbers behind it.

1 · Verify with proxies, not profit

Joseph Buchdahl's analyses of real betting records: ~25% of accounts are still up after 1,000 bets and ~1% after 10,000 — so a profitable year proves almost nothing, and his Bayes-factor math puts strong evidence of skill at roughly 3,500+ bets (Pinnacle series). The working shortcut is closing line value — did you consistently beat the close? — which reaches significance in ~50–few hundred bets because every wager is scored against a benchmark price (Pinnacle on CLV). The forecasting-world analog is a Brier-scored probability log: Good Judgment's superforecasters averaged 0.166 vs 0.259 for regular forecasters, with skill persisting year over year (GJP data). In quant finance the same discipline is the deflated Sharpe ratio — correct your backtest for how many variants you tried before believing it (Bailey & López de Prado 2014).

2 · Know the honest baseline: public models lose to the close

The best-documented open models are calibrated but have no edge against closing lines: FiveThirtyEight's open NFL Elo scored a worse mean squared error than Vegas (13.57 vs 13.10, independent comparison); the classic Dixon-Coles 1997 football model beat 1990s bookmaker margins but that inefficiency is long gone; a rare honest 2026 academic test (xG model, 11 Bundesliga seasons) found the bookmaker better calibrated than the model even while a simulated best-price strategy showed unstable paper profits (Wilkens). A fitted model on public data is, by default, already in the price.

3 · Realistic magnitudes

Verified long-term value bettors cluster at 3–7% ROI per bet (Pinnacle-verified tipsters with 1,000–7,000-pick records); EV-screen tools report ~2% average per trade across hundreds of thousands of user trades (Trademate). Mature stat-arb sleeves discuss Sharpe ~1.5–2.5 at deliberately capped size. Anyone marketing 20%+ per bet is selling something. And the venue responds: soft/US books limit consistent winners, so deployment routes to winners-welcome venues — Pinnacle, Asian brokers, exchanges, prediction markets.

4 · Size with fractional Kelly

Full Kelly maximizes growth but carries a ~1/3 chance of halving your bankroll before doubling; half-Kelly cuts that to ~11% while keeping ~75% of the growth rate — and, crucially, fractional sizing hedges the near-certainty that your edge estimate is too optimistic (why practitioners default to ¼–½ Kelly).

5 · The documented wins all bought a structural advantage

  • Private measurement: the 2024 Polymarket "French whale" commissioned his own neighbor polls in swing states — asking who respondents' neighbors would vote for — and turned ~$30M staked into ~$85M (60 Minutes). One data point, but the archetype is exact: pay for information the marginal dollar doesn't have. n = 1 — would have read as recklessness had it lost
  • Speed into young markets: Polymarket's #1 trader ("Domer", 5,000+ markets) describes deliberately hunting brand-new markets where no one has an information edge yet and research speed wins (interview).
  • Alt-data in finance: satellite parking-lot counts predicted retailer earnings surprises 1–2 weeks ahead and hedge funds traded it profitably (Berkeley Haas); Wall Street alt-data spend is ~$3B/yr.
  • Owning the flow: commodity trading houses out-forecast futures because they see the physical cargoes; airlines/hotels out-forecast their own demand because they see the bookings (5–10% revenue lift attributed to revenue-management forecasting). "Beat the market in your own domain" is the most durable version of the edge.

6 · Expect the edge to shrink as it succeeds

Berk & Green's canonical result: skilled managers attract capital until decreasing returns to scale push net alpha to zero — the skill ends up captured as fees and fund size, not persistent outperformance (JPE 2004). The betting version is account limits; the prediction-market version is moving your own price. Capacity planning isn't pessimism, it's the model.

Verdict: can prediction beat the market?

  1. Yes — but locally, rarely, and self-extinguishingly. Documented wins share a structure: private data, biased flow, or young/thin markets. Every win recruits imitators who shrink it (Grossman-Stiglitz in action).
  2. The market is your best free model. The professional posture is market-as-prior: your model earns a tilt, sized by proven track record — not a replacement forecast. If your process doesn't start from the price, it's not humble enough to survive.
  3. Proof is expensive; proxies are mandatory. Thousands of bets to prove a small edge directly — so practitioners live on closing line value and calibration scores instead, and treat unproven backtests as noise.
  4. Pick your market by its weakness, not your strength. The same model skill that loses in deep markets wins in shallow ones. Beating the S&P is a different sport from beating a three-day-old Kalshi contract, and the evidence table above shows exactly which sport is winnable.
  5. The endgame is becoming the market. The biggest documented winners (Renaissance, Starlizard, revenue-management desks) graduated from predicting prices to setting them — at which point everyone else is trying to beat them.
